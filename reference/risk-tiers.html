Reference · not a stage

Risk-tier schedule

Define assigns a risk tier and calls it a purchase order for rigour. Plan refuses to start without one. Until this page existed the guide printed no price list: four tier names existed on the work-package form and nothing anywhere said what any of them bought.

The four names below are the ones the work-package form already offers, so the form and the guide agree rather than describing two scales. Every obligation is drawn from a rule the stage manuals already state; none of it is new machinery.

THE ORDERING IS THE WHOLE POINT

A tier chosen once the evidence is in hand can only be a tier that evidence satisfies.

01

Where the tier comes from

Assigned once, at Define, by a named role

Assigned at Define

The risk owner records the tier and its reasoning before planning begins, and may not revise it once verification evidence exists. It is one of Define’s nine artifacts and one of its nine gate conditions.

Blocking at Plan

Plan treats an assigned tier as an entry criterion. A blank tier stops planning outright, which is what makes the assignment unavoidable rather than advisory.

Spent at Assure

The tier decides how much scrutiny the work must survive. The layers themselves, and the conditions under which each applies, are defined on the Assure manual and are not restated here.

The tier adds; the change’s own nature decides what applies

Each assurance layer carries its own “applies when” condition, keyed to what the change touches. The tier is keyed to what it costs to be wrong. Both have to be satisfied, and they compose in one direction only: a tier can remove the written-reason skip from a layer that already applies, and at the top tier it can make a layer apply that otherwise would not. It can never excuse a layer whose condition is met.

Two pages disagree about rehearsed rollback, and this row reads them together

Define says the tier decides whether reversal must be described, or described and rehearsed. Release’s entry criteria require rehearsal of everything that reaches it — “not designed, exercised, at least once, somewhere.” Both sentences are in the guide. The row above takes Release’s floor as binding at every tier and lets the tier grade how specific the rehearsal has to be: at Low it may be a path exercised on some earlier occasion, at High it is this change’s reversal, run and recorded. If that reading is wrong, one of the two pages needs correcting, and this is where the disagreement was noticed.

A skip is a decision, at every tier

Where a tier still permits a layer to be skipped, the reason is written into the evidence manifest as declared non-coverage. An unmentioned omission is not a skip; it is a gap, and it is the one thing the schedule cannot price.

Layers, tier by tier

Which of the six are mandatory

Why L4 and L5 harden first

Assure singles them out as the layers cheapest to omit and most expensive to omit wrongly, so they are the first to lose their waiver as the tier rises. That is a restatement of a judgement the Assure manual already makes, not a new one.

04

A name used twice

Two different scales share four words

The guide’s front page carries a Risk SLA using the same four words — Critical, High, Medium, Low — to set how fast a security finding must be remediated: block release, seven days, thirty days, planned backlog. That scale grades a discovered vulnerability. This one grades a unit of work before it starts.

  • A Critical work package is not a critical vulnerability, and neither implies the other.
  • A Low package can still surface a Critical finding, which is remediated on the vulnerability scale regardless of the package’s tier.
  • Nothing in the guide states the two are different, which is why a reader meeting the second one first will read this schedule as a remediation timetable.
Say which scale you mean

The work-package form labels its field Risk class. Where a record could be read either way, write the field name, not the bare word.

05

What this does not do

Provenance, and the edges

Every cell above traces to a rule already written on a stage manual. The tiering — which obligation attaches at which of the four names — is this page’s contribution, and it is the only part that is new.

  • The four obligation types are Define’s own list of what the tier buys: the assurance burden, who must sign, whether a decision record is owed, and the rollback obligation.
  • The six layers, their conditions, and the judgement that L4 and L5 are the ones to justify skipping come from Assure.
  • The independence rule — that a higher tier forbids the implementing party from also verifying — is Define’s sentence, and the separation-of-duty rule behind the Critical row is the front page’s.
  • Rehearsed rollback, the exercised restore and the separately-deployed irreversible migration steps come from Define, Operate and Build respectively.
  • No test enforces any of this. Nothing fails when a High package ships without a rehearsed rollback; the schedule is read by people and by the reviewer, not by CI.
  • The thresholds are a first calibration, not a measured one. No corpus of packages was graded against them, so the boundary between Medium and High is a judgement that should move once there is evidence to move it with.
  • It says nothing about who assigns which tier to which kind of work. The risk owner decides, and the reasoning — not the tier — is what a reviewer should challenge.
